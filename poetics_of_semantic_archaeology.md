# The Poetics of Semantic Archaeology

Semantic Archaeology begins from a simple operation: replacing a word
with the literal image carried by its etymological roots. The
substitution is not intended to define the word. It is intended to
awaken a dormant image.

When "window" becomes "wind eye" or "mortgage" becomes "death pledge,"
the reader briefly encounters English as though it were a foreign
language translated too literally. Ordinary prose becomes strange
without becoming unintelligible.

The project is therefore less about etymology than about recovering
forgotten ways of imagining the world. Many everyday words conceal
traces of bread-making, animal husbandry, astronomy, Roman law, counting
stones, salt, kinship, and vanished technologies. The lexicon seeks
those buried images rather than merely decomposing compounds.

The project also recognizes that history has different degrees of
legibility. Some words preserve their origins clearly. Others require
reconstruction from historical practices. Still others remain unresolved
despite generations of scholarship.

Rather than treating uncertainty as failure, the lexicon treats it as
evidence. A contested origin is itself an archaeological finding.
Sometimes the most truthful substitution is not another phrase but an
acknowledgement that the original image has been lost or remains
disputed.

The aim is not comprehensiveness. It is curation. Every accepted entry
should transform a sentence, however briefly, by recovering an image
that has slept inside the language for centuries.

Recover forgotten images where possible. Represent the limits of
historical knowledge where necessary.
